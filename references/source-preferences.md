# Extracted Source Preferences

The source corpus is the user's local research-figure gallery at `E:/01 论文/01 PKU papers/未来SDG/绘图/图库`. It contains 50 high-level multi-panel figures, mostly from Nature/Science-style climate, SDG, energy, water, agriculture, PV, health, distribution diagnostics, and global-change papers.

## Strongest Preference

The user especially prefers figures with one or two main panels and many supporting satellite panels. The main panel usually establishes the global/spatial/temporal finding. The satellite panels explain the mechanism, regional heterogeneity, country cases, distribution, uncertainty, or fairness dimension.

The user's newer preference is stricter about hierarchy: a figure should not let every panel compete for attention. Before designing, decide what the reader should see first, which panel carries the central claim, and which panels merely explain or validate it. High-level figures often look sophisticated because they control attention, not because every result is equally prominent.

## Narrative Patterns

- Spatial first: large world or regional map, then marginal distributions, ranked bars, Lorenz/fairness curves, and socioeconomic scatter plots.
- Scenario first: small-multiple trajectories or stacked areas, then a map or summary chart that makes the scenario consequences concrete.
- System first: grouped panels by domain such as energy, land, water, air, food, or SDGs, each with the same line-chart grammar.
- Comparison first: paired maps or paired main charts with identical scales, then diagnostic panels explaining why the two systems differ.
- Matrix first: repeated maps or repeated small panels when the goal is systematic comparison across indicators or scenarios.
- Distribution first: ridgelines, box-density-scatter hybrids, marginal histograms, and residual insets when the main claim is about distribution shape, spread, or model fit rather than a single mean.

## Preferred Figure Traits

- High information density with explicit hierarchy, not equal-size chart grids unless systematic comparison is the point.
- Diverse chart types in one figure: maps, stacked areas, bars, scatter plots, distributions, glyphs, boxplots, and small multiples.
- White canvas, light gray gridlines, restrained borders, and small but readable text.
- Harmonized low-saturation palettes with medium-saturation accents.
- Direct labels and short annotations instead of large detached legends.
- Complete scientific metadata: units, scenarios, years, model names, income groups, country labels, confidence bands, and sample-size cues.
- Consistent variable-color mapping across panels.
- Maps are often pale and detailed; supporting charts carry the sharper analytic contrast.
- New gallery additions reinforce: maps can carry inset donut/ring glyphs or histograms in unused space; bars can use solid light-to-dark color families without transparency; scatter panels can be strengthened with marginal distributions, residual insets, and quadrant structure; ridgeline and box-density-scatter panels are preferred when distributions need to be compared compactly.
- The best figures have a controlled reading order. The main panel should be visually dominant through size, placement, or stronger contrast. Supporting panels should be quieter, smaller, or locally grouped so they do not dilute the central message.

## Preferred Panel Roles

- Main map: establishes global spatial pattern.
- Paired main maps: compare technologies, scenarios, or time periods.
- Country micro-panels: show exemplar profiles around a map.
- Marginal strips: summarize latitudinal, longitudinal, scenario, or national distributions.
- Ranking bars: compress total contributions or country order.
- Scatter/quadrant plots: connect physical results to GDP, income, development status, or capacity.
- Distribution diagnostics: Lorenz curves, violins, boxplots, ridgelines, uncertainty ribbons.
- Glyph overlays: donut or ring glyphs on maps when regional composition matters.
- Marginal diagnostics: histograms, density strips, side profiles, and inset residual plots attached to scatter or map panels.
- Hybrid distribution panels: half-violin plus boxplot plus jittered points when category-level spread and sample density both matter.

## Avoid

- Decorative gradients, oversized titles, marketing-page composition, and isolated single charts.
- Saturated rainbow palettes unless the variable is naturally ordinal and the legend is very clear.
- Repeating full legends in every panel.
- Crowding labels on maps without hierarchy.
- Making every panel the same size when there is a clear main finding.
- Using color changes that imply a new variable when they only indicate style.
- Over-brightening every mark. Reserve the highest saturation for the key result, selected pathway, or main comparison; use muted tones for context and diagnostics.
- Adding information just because space exists. If a panel does not guide the reader toward the main claim, simplify it, shrink it, or remove it.
